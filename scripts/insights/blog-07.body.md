## Where AI genuinely earns its place

A lot of AI business cases start in the wrong place.

They start with the technology.

> Where can we use AI?

I would start with the work.

> Where are we spending meaningful time or money on repeatable activity that software could now perform better, faster or more cheaply?

That sounds like a small distinction.

It changes everything.

In one large organisation I worked in, applying AI to content operations removed **£690,000 of annual spend**.

The interesting part was not that an AI model could write something.

The value came from looking at the operating process around content, identifying where external cost and repeated manual work existed, then redesigning the workflow.

That is what Operational AI means to me.

Not adding a chatbot to the homepage.

Changing how the business operates.

## Where AI adds cost without adding value

There are four patterns I would be wary of.

### A solution looking for a problem

Someone sees an impressive demo and starts searching the organisation for somewhere to install it.

That reverses the logic.

Start with an expensive, slow or poor-quality workflow. Then assess whether AI belongs in the solution.

### Buying an AI platform before designing the workflow

Technology does not define ownership, approvals, exceptions or quality standards for you.

If nobody knows what happens when the model is uncertain, who checks sensitive output or where the source data comes from, the platform is not the missing piece.

### Over-engineered personalisation

Generating thousands of personalised messages is easy.

Generating thousands of messages people actually want is harder.

Automation can multiply irrelevance as efficiently as relevance.

### Chatbots that do not solve anything

A conversational interface is useful when conversation is the best way to complete the job.

It is not useful simply because AI makes one possible.

Sometimes a search box, form, dashboard or human conversation is better.

## Five high-value places to investigate

I would not rank these universally because the economics depend on the organisation. But these are five places I would investigate early in many B2B businesses.

### Content operations

Drafting, adaptation, localisation, summarisation, metadata, repurposing and quality checks can contain large amounts of repeatable work.

The opportunity is not "replace writers".

It is to identify which parts require judgement and which parts consume time without adding equivalent value.

### Reporting and analysis

Teams still spend hours exporting data, cleaning spreadsheets, writing weekly commentary and answering recurring questions.

AI can reduce the manual interpretation layer when the underlying data is reliable.

Bad data remains bad data.

### Sales intelligence

Preparing for meetings, summarising account history, extracting themes from calls and identifying missing information can be accelerated.

The salesperson should still own the relationship, negotiation and judgement.

### Workflow triage

Incoming requests, support tickets, operational emails and documents often require classification before a human can act.

AI can read, categorise, extract and route.

That can remove a surprising amount of administrative work.

### Knowledge retrieval

People repeatedly ask questions that the organisation has already answered somewhere.

Policies, product documentation, process guidance and account information become much more useful when staff can retrieve the right information quickly.

The hard part is permissions, source quality and trust.

Not the chat interface.

## What to try first if you have zero AI in your stack

Do not launch ten pilots.

Pick one workflow.

For 30 days, choose something that is:

- repeated frequently
- measurable
- currently expensive in time or money
- based on information you can access legitimately
- low enough risk to test safely
- capable of retaining human review where needed

Map the current process first.

How many times does it happen?

Who touches it?

How long does each step take?

What does it cost?

Where does quality fail?

Where do people wait?

Then redesign it with AI as one possible component.

The output of the exercise may be that automation is unnecessary.

That is a good result if it prevents an expensive distraction.

## Measuring actual AI ROI

AI ROI becomes meaningless when the baseline is invented after implementation.

Measure before you change the process.

I would focus on three broad categories.

### Cost

What cash cost actually disappears or reduces?

Licences, outsourced services, contractor spend, processing cost and avoided incremental hiring can be measurable.

Be careful with theoretical salary savings.

Saving an employee ten minutes does not mean the business saved ten minutes of salary unless that capacity becomes useful elsewhere.

### Time

How much elapsed time or human effort does the workflow require before and after?

Both matter.

A process can use the same human effort but complete two days faster because waiting has been removed.

### Quality and risk

Did error rates change?

Did consistency improve?

Did customer response times improve?

Did the system introduce new review, privacy or compliance risk?

A cheaper process that produces worse outcomes is not necessarily efficient.

## Redesign before you cut

There is a dangerous sequence emerging in some businesses:

1. Buy AI.
2. Assume large productivity gains.
3. Cut people.
4. Discover the workflow still needs the judgement and exception handling those people provided.

Reverse it.

Understand the work.

Redesign the process.

Automate what should be automated.

Measure the outcome.

Then decide what the operating model should look like.

AI can absolutely reduce cost.

But "we use AI" is not an efficiency strategy.

The business case lives in the workflow.
