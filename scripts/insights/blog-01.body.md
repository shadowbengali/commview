## The buyer we designed the stack for

A lot of modern B2B marketing still runs on an operating model designed for a different buyer.

The journey was relatively easy to describe. Someone searched for a problem, found your website, downloaded something, entered a nurture programme, accumulated a score and eventually crossed a threshold. Marketing handed the lead to Sales. Sales qualified it. An opportunity appeared in the CRM.

The technology reflected that journey.

Marketing automation platforms scored people. CRM systems tracked contacts, leads and opportunities. Attribution models tried to connect campaigns to pipeline. Dashboards showed MQLs, SQLs, conversion rates and revenue.

There is nothing inherently wrong with any of those things.

I have built and run versions of that system myself. At scale, MQLs can still be useful. A person who repeatedly engages with relevant content is giving you information. A demo request is still a strong signal. A contact who fits your ICP and starts researching a problem is still worth understanding.

The mistake is treating that individual signal as if it explains how the account is buying.

That is where the traditional B2B GTM model starts to break.

## What actually changed in the B2B buyer journey

The buyer became more capable.

A few years ago, if I was evaluating a complex B2B product, I might search Google, read vendor websites, look at analyst reports, browse forums, download a few guides and eventually speak to suppliers.

Now I can give an AI system the problem.

I can ask it to help define requirements. I can give it constraints. I can ask it to compare approaches, identify vendors, build a shortlist, challenge assumptions and create questions for the sales call.

That changes the role of marketing and Sales.

The buyer does not necessarily need a supplier to explain the category to them. They may arrive at the first conversation with a much better understanding of the available options than they would have had five years ago.

At the same time, the buying process inside the account is rarely represented by one person.

The person reading your technical documentation may not be the person who first found you. The person asking an AI assistant to compare vendors may never fill in a form. Procurement may arrive late. Security may appear halfway through. Finance may kill a deal after weeks of positive conversations.

Yet a lot of GTM reporting still tries to reduce that behaviour to:

**Lead to MQL to SQL to Opportunity to Won**

Useful? Yes.

A complete representation of the buying process? No.

## Where the current GTM stack is breaking

The problem is not that every tool suddenly became useless. The problem is that we ask the tools to tell us things they were never designed to know.

### Lead scoring is too narrow when it becomes the truth

A lead score can tell you that a person has done things you decided were valuable.

It cannot automatically tell you whether the account is genuinely moving towards a purchase.

A highly engaged junior employee doing research can score beautifully and never influence a buying decision. A senior buyer can arrive with a defined requirement, visit three pages, book a meeting and become the best opportunity of the quarter.

Keep lead scoring if it helps.

Just stop pretending the score is the customer.

### Attribution becomes less believable as research spreads

The more places a buyer researches, the less credible a neat single-channel explanation becomes.

A customer may discover a problem through a colleague, research it with AI, see your name in a community, visit your website directly, read three articles, disappear for six weeks, return through branded search and then book a meeting.

Which channel created the opportunity?

The honest answer may be: several things contributed, and your analytics can only see some of them.

That does not make measurement pointless. It means measurement needs to become more commercially mature.

### Sales enters a different conversation

If buyers arrive better informed, the first sales conversation changes too.

Repeating the website back to them is not valuable.

The salesperson needs to understand context, uncover constraints, challenge assumptions and help multiple stakeholders make a decision.

That means the handoff between Marketing and Sales should contain more than a score and a contact record.

What has the account been researching? Who else is involved? What changed? What problem appears to be driving the activity? What does the buyer already know?

That is a much more useful starting point.

## The MQL isn't dead. The linear model is.

There has been a tendency in B2B marketing to declare things dead.

The funnel is dead. The MQL is dead. Email is dead. SEO is dead.

Usually, the reality is less dramatic.

The MQL can still be useful. What needs updating is the idea that **one score, one handoff and one linear journey can explain how a B2B account buys.**

I would keep person-level engagement, but put it inside a broader commercial picture.

That picture should combine four types of signal.

### Person-level engagement

Who is actually interacting with you?

Forms, events, product usage, email engagement, conversations and high-intent website actions still matter.

### Account-level intent

Is activity spreading across the organisation? Are several people researching related subjects? Is engagement increasing or becoming more commercially specific?

### Buying-group behaviour

Who appears to be involved in the decision? Do you have technical, commercial and operational stakeholders? Are you dependent on one enthusiastic contact?

### Opportunity and revenue outcomes

What actually happened?

Which accounts progressed? Which stalled? Which converted? Which expanded? Which churned?

This last layer matters because GTM teams can become very good at optimising the metrics their systems make easiest to see.

The purpose of GTM is not to generate beautifully scored records.

It is to create and convert demand profitably.

## What a modern B2B GTM strategy needs

I would not start by buying another platform.

Start by changing the questions.

Instead of:

> How many MQLs did Marketing create?

Ask:

> Which target accounts are showing meaningful buying behaviour, and what evidence do we have?

Instead of:

> Which campaign gets the opportunity credit?

Ask:

> Which activities repeatedly appear in accounts that progress?

Instead of:

> Did Sales follow up the MQL?

Ask:

> Did we give Sales enough context to have a useful conversation?

Instead of treating Marketing, Product and Sales as three sequential functions, connect the information they hold.

Product understands usage and value.

Marketing understands attention and demand.

Sales understands objections, stakeholders and commercial reality.

Customer teams understand adoption, expansion and churn.

The GTM system should make those signals more useful together.

## How to test whether your GTM stack is broken

You do not need a transformation programme to find out.

Ask five questions.

**1. Can we explain why our best opportunities progress?**

Not which campaign touched them. Why they progress.

**2. Can Sales see useful buying context before speaking to an account?**

If all they receive is name, email, company and MQL score, probably not.

**3. Can we see account behaviour as well as individual behaviour?**

If six people from the same company are researching you and your system treats them as six unrelated leads, you are missing part of the picture.

**4. Do Marketing and Sales use the same definition of quality?**

If Marketing celebrates volume while Sales complains about relevance, the problem is not necessarily either team. The operating model may be measuring different things.

**5. Can we connect GTM activity to pipeline and revenue without pretending every sale has one source?**

If not, fix the measurement model before adding more channels.

The answer is not to rip everything out.

It is to stop forcing today's buyer into yesterday's journey.

The stack should serve the buying process.

The buying process should not have to fit the stack.
