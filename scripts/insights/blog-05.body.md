## The three questions before "how do we build it?"

Product teams often start one question too late.

A customer asks for a feature.

Sales says it could unlock a deal.

A competitor launches something similar.

Someone senior likes the idea.

The conversation immediately becomes:

> How difficult would this be to build?

Before that, ask three questions.

### Who is this actually for?

One customer?

A recognisable segment?

Most of the installed base?

A buyer rather than a user?

An internal team?

Specificity matters because "customers want this" is often shorthand for "a customer asked for this".

### What changes for them?

What can the user do after the feature exists that they cannot do now?

Does it remove a painful workaround?

Save meaningful time?

Reduce risk?

Unlock a job they currently cannot complete?

If you cannot describe the changed behaviour, the feature is still an idea rather than a validated requirement.

### What changes for the business?

Will it improve conversion?

Retention?

Expansion?

Activation?

Cost to serve?

Strategic differentiation?

Sometimes the user value is real but the commercial value is too small to justify the opportunity cost.

Product strategy sits in that trade-off.

## Signals a feature will genuinely move the needle

No signal guarantees success, but some are much stronger than others.

### The same underlying problem appears repeatedly

Five customers asking for five slightly different features may actually be describing one problem.

That is stronger evidence than five identical requests copied from a competitor's product.

### The problem appears in wins, losses or churn

If the same limitation repeatedly blocks deals or contributes to customers leaving, it deserves investigation.

Do not immediately build the requested solution.

Validate the underlying cause first.

### The capability strengthens what the product already does well

Features adjacent to the core value proposition are often easier to explain, adopt and support than features that pull the product into a different category.

### Users already have a workaround

Workarounds are useful evidence.

Spreadsheets, manual exports, duplicate data entry and awkward integrations tell you that users are already spending effort to solve the problem.

That does not prove your proposed feature is right.

It proves the problem may be valuable.

## Signals it is a distraction

### One loud customer asked for it

Important customers deserve attention.

They do not automatically deserve control of your roadmap.

### Sales lost one deal and blamed the missing feature

Lost-deal feedback matters, but buyers give many reasons for saying no.

Was the missing capability genuinely decisive? Would the prospect have bought if it existed? Does the requirement repeat elsewhere?

### A competitor launched it

Competitor activity is evidence, not instruction.

You do not know why they built it, whether customers use it or whether it supports their strategy.

### It came out of a strategy offsite

A room full of intelligent people can create very convincing solutions to problems nobody has validated.

Discovery should happen before commitment.

## The shadow of the loudest customer

Large customers create gravity.

They spend more. Senior people know their names. Their account teams have access to leadership. When they request something, the request travels quickly.

That is rational.

The danger comes when one customer's operating model starts becoming your product strategy.

Imagine your strongest customer asks for a complex approval workflow.

Building it may protect significant revenue.

But before putting it on the core roadmap, ask:

Is this a common need in the segment we want to win?

Does it strengthen our proposition?

Would other customers use it?

Could it be solved through configuration, services or integration instead?

What will not get built if we do this?

Sometimes the right decision is still to build it.

The point is to make the trade-off consciously.

## Prioritisation frameworks that survive real trade-offs

Frameworks help because product decisions are emotional.

They force teams to expose assumptions.

### RICE

RICE considers Reach, Impact, Confidence and Effort.

It is useful when you have enough data to make the inputs meaningful and several competing initiatives need comparison.

Its weakness is false precision.

If Reach is guessed, Impact is subjective and Confidence is optimistic, multiplying the numbers does not make the answer scientific.

### Value vs effort

Simple and useful.

Map initiatives against expected value and delivery effort.

It quickly exposes low-value, high-effort work.

Its weakness is that "value" can hide several things. Customer value, revenue, retention and strategic value are not always interchangeable.

### ICE

Impact, Confidence and Ease is fast and lightweight.

It is useful earlier in discovery when you want to compare hypotheses without pretending you have perfect data.

Again, the score is a conversation aid.

Not the decision.

## What Commview would actually put on the roadmap

I would use a framework to structure the evidence, then apply judgement.

A simple roadmap decision might include:

**Problem:** What user or customer problem are we solving?

**Evidence:** What tells us it is real and repeatable?

**Audience:** Who experiences it?

**Expected customer impact:** What changes if we solve it?

**Expected business impact:** What commercial metric should move?

**Confidence:** What do we know versus assume?

**Effort and dependency:** What does solving it displace?

**Validation plan:** What is the cheapest way to learn before committing fully?

That gives leadership something more useful than a score of 83 versus 79.

The purpose of prioritisation is not to make disagreement disappear.

It is to make the reasons for the decision visible.
