Why your pipeline problem is usually a positioning problem

Pipeline is the symptom everyone can see. It is rarely where the fault sits — and the fix almost never lives in the channel you are blaming.

In short

  • Thin pipeline is a symptom. Positioning, offer and reach are the three things underneath it.
  • Drop-off location tells you which one you have — before the meeting, or after it.
  • Fix the message on existing spend before you add budget or headcount.

Every scale-up we meet arrives with the same sentence. Pipeline is not where it needs to be. It is said with certainty, because it is the one number everyone in the business can see, and it is the number the board asks about first.

It is almost never the problem. It is the place the problem becomes visible, which is a different thing. Treating it as the problem is how businesses end up spending more on the channel that is working hardest to overcome a message that is not working at all.

The symptom everyone agrees on

Ask four people in the same business why pipeline is thin and you will get four answers, each of them true from where that person sits. Marketing points at budget. Sales points at lead quality. Product points at the roadmap. The founder points at the market.

None of them are lying, and none of them are looking at the same evidence. That disagreement is the first useful signal: when a business cannot agree on the cause, the cause is usually upstream of all of them.

When four teams disagree about the cause, the cause is usually upstream of all four.

Three things that look like pipeline

In our experience there are three distinct faults that all present as thin pipeline, and they need completely different responses:

  1. Reach. The right people are not seeing you often enough. This is the only one that more budget genuinely fixes.
  2. Relevance. They are seeing you and not recognising themselves in what you say. This is positioning.
  3. Offer. They recognise themselves, believe you, and still cannot justify the first step. This is commercial design.

Spend follows the first diagnosis anyone makes, which is why so much of it lands on reach. Reach is the most expensive of the three to fix and the least likely to be the fault.

How to tell which one you have

The diagnostic is cheaper than it sounds. You are looking for where people stop, not how many.

A quick version you can run this week

Take the last twenty opportunities that did not close. Write one sentence per opportunity describing the moment it went cold. Sort the sentences into the four rows above. The pile with the most sentences in it is your actual problem, and it will rarely be the one currently receiving the most budget.

What we would do

Run the twenty-opportunity sort before you approve next quarter's budget. It takes an afternoon and it changes what the budget is for.

What we would do first

Positioning work has a reputation for being slow and abstract. It does not have to be. The version that moves numbers is narrow: one sentence about who it is for, one about what changes for them, and one about why you rather than the obvious alternative.

Everything else — the deck, the site, the sequences — is that sentence repeated in different places. If the sentence is wrong, repeating it more loudly is the most expensive thing you can do.

Questions we get asked about this

How do I know whether it is positioning or demand generation?

Look at where prospects drop out. If they book and then go quiet, they did not understand what you do. If they never book at all, the problem is further up — reach, relevance or offer.

How long does repositioning take to show up in pipeline?

Messaging changes show up in meeting quality within a few weeks. Pipeline value lags by roughly one sales cycle, so for most B2B scale-ups that is one to two quarters before the numbers move.

Can we fix this without pausing demand generation?

Yes, and you should. Repositioning is a change to what the same spend says, not a reason to stop spending. Run the new message on a slice of budget first and compare.
